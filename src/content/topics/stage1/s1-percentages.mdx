<WhyItMatters>

Discounts, interest rates, exam marks, tax and population growth are all given as percentages. Percentages make amounts easy to compare because they always use the same denominator, 100.

This topic gives one method, the multiplier, that handles finding a percentage, increasing, decreasing and even working backwards from a changed amount.

</WhyItMatters>

<Explanation>

<Section id="what-a-percentage-is" title="Out of a hundred">

Per cent means "out of a hundred". A <Term id="percentage" /> is a number of hundredths, so it can be written as a fraction or a decimal, as in <Prereq id="s1-decimals" />:

<Claim display tex="35\% = \frac{35}{100} = 0.35" />

To turn a percentage into a decimal, divide by 100. Some percentages are worth knowing as fractions:

<Claim display tex="50\% = \frac{1}{2}" />

<Claim display tex="25\% = \frac{1}{4}" />

<Claim display tex="10\% = \frac{1}{10}" />

A percentage can be more than 100. Then it is more than the whole amount:

<Claim display tex="150\% = 1.5" />

</Section>

<Section id="percentage-of-an-amount" title="A percentage of an amount">

"Of" means multiply, as with fractions in <Prereq id="s1-multiply-divide-fractions" />. So to find 35% of 80, multiply 80 by 35 hundredths:

<Claim display tex="0.35 \times 80 = 28" />

Without a calculator, build the percentage from easy pieces. Ten per cent of 80 is <Claim tex="0.1 \times 80 = 8" />, and five per cent is half of that, <Claim tex="8 \div 2 = 4" />. So 35% is three tens and a five:

<Claim display tex="8 + 8 + 8 + 4 = 28" />

<Misconception id="percentage-as-number" />

To write one amount as a percentage of another, make the fraction part over whole, then multiply by 100 to count hundredths. Eighteen out of forty:

<Claim display tex="\frac{18}{40} \times 100 = 45" />

So 18 is 45% of 40.

</Section>

<Section id="increase-and-decrease" title="Increasing and decreasing">

Increasing 80 by 15% means keeping all of the 80, which is 100% of it, and adding 15% more. The new amount is 115% of the old one. As a decimal, 115% is 1.15: this number is called the <Term id="multiplier" />.

<Claim display tex="80 \times 1.15 = 92" />

A decrease works the same way. Decreasing by 15% leaves 85% of the amount, so the multiplier is 0.85:

<Claim display tex="80 \times 0.85 = 68" />

<Callout kind="idea">

For an increase of p%, multiply by $1 + \frac{p}{100}$. For a decrease of p%, multiply by $1 - \frac{p}{100}$.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="reverse-percentages" title="Working backwards">

Sometimes you know the amount after a change and need the amount before it. A coat costs £72 after a 20% increase. The new price is the old price times the multiplier 1.2. To undo multiplying by 1.2, divide by 1.2:

<Claim display tex="72 \div 1.2 = 60" />

Check: 20% of 60 is <Claim tex="0.2 \times 60 = 12" />, and <Claim tex="60 + 12 = 72" />.

<Misconception id="reverse-percentage-of-new" />

Taking 20% off 72 gives a different number, because 20% of 72 is more than 20% of 60:

<Claim display tex="72 \times 0.8 = 57.6" />

</Section>

<Practice />

<Takeaways>

- A percentage is a number of hundredths: divide by 100 to get the decimal.
- To find a percentage of an amount, multiply the amount by the decimal.
- To write A as a percentage of B, work out A over B and multiply by 100.
- Increase by p%: multiply by $1 + \frac{p}{100}$. Decrease by p%: multiply by $1 - \frac{p}{100}$.
- To find the amount before a change, divide the new amount by the multiplier. Never take the percentage of the new amount.

</Takeaways>
