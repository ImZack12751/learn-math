<WhyItMatters>

Fractions are simplified, added and compared using factors and multiples. Later, algebra is factorised in exactly the same way that numbers are factorised here.

Prime numbers are the building blocks: every whole number greater than 1 is made by multiplying primes together, in exactly one way. Once you can see that structure, finding common factors and common multiples becomes a matter of reading it off.

</WhyItMatters>

<Explanation>

<Section id="factors-and-multiples" title="Factors and multiples">

A <Term id="factor" /> of a number divides into it exactly, with nothing left over. Because <Claim tex="3 \times 4 = 12" />, both 3 and 4 are factors of 12. Factors come in pairs that multiply to the number:

<Claim display tex="12 = 1 \times 12 = 2 \times 6 = 3 \times 4" />

So the factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12.

A <Term id="multiple" /> of a number is that number times a whole number. The multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and so on, without end.

The two ideas are opposite views of one fact. Because 12 is $3 \times 4$, the number 4 is a factor of 12, and 12 is a multiple of 4.

<Callout kind="note">

A factor of a number is never bigger than the number. A multiple of a number is never smaller than it.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="primes" title="Prime numbers">

Some numbers have only two factors: 1 and themselves. These are the <Term id="prime-number">prime numbers</Term>:

<Claim display tex="2 < 3 < 5 < 7 < 11 < 13 < 17 < 19" />

The number 1 is not prime, because it has only one factor. The number 2 is the only even prime: every other even number has 2 as a factor as well.

A number with more than two factors, such as 12, is called composite. It can be broken into smaller factors.

</Section>

<Section id="prime-factorisation" title="Breaking a number into primes">

Keep breaking a composite number into factors until every factor is prime. A tidy way is to divide by the smallest prime that goes exactly, again and again:

<Claim display tex="72 = 2 \times 36 = 2 \times 2 \times 18 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 9 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3" />

Repeated factors are written as a <Term id="power" />, using the <Term id="exponent" /> to count them. This is the <Term id="prime-factorisation" /> of 72:

<Claim display tex="72 = 2^{3} \times 3^{2}" />

Whichever factors you split off first, you always end with the same primes. That is why the prime factorisation describes a number completely: it is like the list of ingredients the number is made from.

<Callout kind="idea">

To find a prime factorisation: divide by 2 as many times as you can, then by 3, then 5, then 7, and so on, until what is left is prime. Write repeated primes as powers.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="hcf-and-lcm" title="Common factors and common multiples">

The <Term id="hcf" /> of two numbers is the largest number that divides both. Look at the prime factorisations of 12 and 18:

<Claim display tex="12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3" />

<Claim display tex="18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3" />

Any common factor must be built only from primes that both numbers contain. They share one 2 and one 3, so the biggest common factor is

<Claim display tex="2 \times 3 = 6" />

The <Term id="lcm" /> of two numbers is the smallest number that both divide into. It must contain all the primes of each number. Start with the primes of 12, then add the primes of 18 that are not already there. The 2 and one 3 are covered; one more 3 is needed:

<Claim display tex="2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 36" />

<Callout kind="idea">

HCF: multiply the primes the numbers share. LCM: multiply every prime that appears in either number, each as many times as it appears in the number that has it most.

</Callout>

<Misconception id="hcf-lcm-confused" />

<Misconception id="lcm-as-product" />

The two are linked. For any two numbers, the HCF times the LCM equals the two numbers multiplied together:

<Claim display tex="6 \times 36 = 12 \times 18" />

</Section>

</Explanation>

<Practice />

<Takeaways>

- A factor divides a number exactly; a multiple is the number times a whole number.
- A prime number has exactly two factors, 1 and itself. The number 1 is not prime, and 2 is the only even prime.
- Every whole number greater than 1 has one prime factorisation. Find it by dividing by the smallest prime that goes, again and again.
- HCF: multiply the primes the numbers share. LCM: multiply every prime from either number, each as many times as the number that has it most.
- The product of two numbers is a common multiple, but not always the lowest one.

</Takeaways>
