<WhyItMatters>

Prices, measurements and calculator answers are written as decimals. Decimals and fractions are two ways of writing the same numbers, and moving between them lets you pick whichever is easier for the job in front of you.

Everything here comes from one idea, place value, which you already use for whole numbers.

</WhyItMatters>

<Explanation>

<Section id="place-value" title="Place value after the point">

In a whole number, each column is worth ten times the column to its right: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands. This is <Term id="place-value" />. Carry the pattern on to the right of the ones column, and each new column is worth a tenth of the one before it: tenths, hundredths, thousandths.

A decimal point marks where the ones column ends. So a <Term id="decimal" /> such as 3.25 means 3 ones, 2 tenths and 5 hundredths:

<Claim display tex="3.25 = 3 + \frac{2}{10} + \frac{5}{100}" />

Adding zeros at the end of a decimal changes nothing, because they add no tenths or hundredths:

<Claim display tex="3.5 = 3.50 = 3.500" />

</Section>

<Section id="decimals-to-fractions" title="From a decimal to a fraction">

Read the decimal by its last column. The last digit of 0.35 is in the hundredths column, so 0.35 is 35 hundredths. Then simplify, as in <Prereq id="s1-simplifying-fractions" />:

<Claim display tex="0.35 = \frac{35}{100} = \frac{7}{20}" />

The number of decimal places tells you the power of ten: one place means tenths, two means hundredths, three means thousandths.

<Misconception id="decimal-wrong-power-of-ten" />

</Section>

<Section id="fractions-to-decimals" title="From a fraction to a decimal">

Go the other way by finding an equivalent fraction whose denominator is 10, 100 or 1000. For three eighths, multiply the top and the bottom by 125, because <Claim tex="8 \times 125 = 1000" />:

<Claim display tex="\frac{3}{8} = \frac{3 \times 125}{8 \times 125} = \frac{375}{1000} = 0.375" />

This works whenever the denominator, in its simplest form, has only 2s and 5s as prime factors, since <Claim tex="10 = 2 \times 5" />. Other fractions, such as one third, give decimals that never end; those are met later.

A fraction is also a division, so dividing the numerator by the denominator gives the same decimal.

<Misconception id="fraction-digits-as-decimal" />

</Section>

<Section id="adding-decimals" title="Adding and subtracting decimals">

Only digits with the same place value can be added: tenths to tenths, hundredths to hundredths. So line up the decimal points. Writing zeros at the end to give both numbers the same number of decimal places can help:

<Claim display tex="3.5 + 1.25 = 3.50 + 1.25 = 4.75" />

<Misconception id="decimal-add-misaligned" />

</Section>

<Section id="multiplying-decimals" title="Multiplying decimals">

A tenth of a tenth is a hundredth. So when two numbers with one decimal place each are multiplied, the answer is in hundredths:

<Claim display tex="0.3 \times 0.4 = \frac{3}{10} \times \frac{4}{10} = \frac{12}{100} = 0.12" />

<Callout kind="idea">

To multiply decimals, multiply the numbers as if there were no points. Then count the decimal places in both numbers altogether; the answer has that many decimal places.

</Callout>

<Claim display tex="1.2 \times 0.05 = 0.06" />

There, <Claim tex="12 \times 5 = 60" />, and there are 3 decimal places in total, giving 0.060, which is 0.06.

</Section>

<Practice />

<Takeaways>

- After the decimal point the columns are tenths, hundredths, thousandths: each a tenth of the column before.
- Decimal to fraction: put the digits over 10, 100 or 1000 to match the last column, then simplify.
- Fraction to decimal: find an equivalent fraction over 10, 100 or 1000, or divide the numerator by the denominator.
- To add or subtract, line up the decimal points.
- To multiply, multiply without the points, then give the answer as many decimal places as the two numbers have altogether.

</Takeaways>
