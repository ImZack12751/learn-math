<WhyItMatters>

Ratios describe mixtures and comparisons: paint mixed 2 parts blue to 3 parts white, a map scale of 1 to 25 000, a class of boys and girls. Proportion is what lets you scale a recipe, convert a currency or work out the cost of a different number of items.

Both rest on the same idea as equivalent fractions: multiplying every part by the same number changes the amounts but not the comparison.

</WhyItMatters>

<Explanation>

<Section id="what-a-ratio-is" title="What a ratio is">

A <Term id="ratio" /> compares amounts of the same kind. If a drink is made with 2 cups of juice for every 3 cups of water, the ratio of juice to water is 2 : 3, read "2 to 3".

The order matters: juice to water is 2 : 3, but water to juice is 3 : 2. The parts of the ratio follow the order the amounts are named in.

</Section>

<Section id="simplifying-ratios" title="Simplifying ratios">

Doubling the recipe gives 4 cups of juice and 6 of water. The drink tastes the same, so the ratios are equal:

<Claim display tex="4 : 6 = 2 : 3" />

Just as with <Prereq id="s1-simplifying-fractions" />, multiplying or dividing every part by the same number keeps a ratio the same. A ratio is in its simplest form when the parts are whole numbers with no common factor. Divide every part by the <Term id="hcf" />:

<Claim display tex="12 : 18 = 2 : 3" />

There, the HCF of 12 and 18 is 6. A ratio can have more than two parts; the same rule applies to every part:

<Claim display tex="30 : 42 : 24 = 5 : 7 : 4" />

If the parts are decimals, multiply every part by 10 (or 100) first to make whole numbers:

<Claim display tex="1.5 : 2.5 = 15 : 25 = 3 : 5" />

If the parts are in different units, change them into the same unit first. Forty centimetres to 2 metres is 40 cm to 200 cm:

<Claim display tex="40 : 200 = 1 : 5" />

<Misconception id="fraction-mixed-units" />

</Section>

<Section id="sharing-in-a-ratio" title="Sharing in a ratio">

To share £60 in the ratio 2 : 3, think of the money as split into equal parts: 2 parts for the first person and 3 for the second, which is 5 parts in all.

<Claim display tex="2 + 3 = 5" />

<Claim display tex="60 \div 5 = 12" />

One part is £12, so the shares are 2 parts and 3 parts:

<Claim display tex="2 \times 12 = 24" />

<Claim display tex="3 \times 12 = 36" />

Check that the shares add up to the whole amount: <Claim tex="24 + 36 = 60" />.

<Misconception id="share-divide-by-part" />

</Section>

<Section id="direct-proportion" title="Direct proportion">

Two quantities are in <Term id="direct-proportion" /> when doubling one doubles the other, tripling one triples the other, and so on. The cost of items at a fixed price is the standard example.

The unitary method finds the value of one first, then multiplies. If 5 pens cost £3.40, one pen costs

<Claim display tex="3.40 \div 5 = 0.68" />

so 8 pens cost

<Claim display tex="0.68 \times 8 = 5.44" />

</Section>

<Practice />

<Takeaways>

- A ratio compares amounts in a fixed order: a : b is not the same as b : a.
- Multiplying or dividing every part by the same number keeps a ratio the same. Simplest form uses whole numbers with no common factor.
- Put both parts in the same unit, and clear decimals, before simplifying.
- To share in a ratio: add the parts, divide the amount by that total to find one part, then multiply by each share's number of parts.
- In direct proportion, find the value of one, then multiply. Never add the difference.

</Takeaways>
