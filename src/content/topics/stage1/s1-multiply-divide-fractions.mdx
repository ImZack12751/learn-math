<WhyItMatters>

"Two thirds of four fifths" and "how many quarter-hour slots fit in two and a half hours" are multiplications and divisions of fractions. They appear in recipes, scale drawings, probability and every formula that contains a fraction.

Multiplying fractions turns out to be simpler than adding them: no common denominator is needed. Dividing is then one extra step, using the reciprocal.

</WhyItMatters>

<Explanation>

<Section id="multiplying" title="Multiplying fractions">

Multiplying by a fraction means taking that fraction of something. So $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}$ means two thirds of four fifths.

Picture a bar cut into fifths with 4 shaded. Now cut every fifth into thirds. The whole is now 15 equal pieces, and two thirds of each shaded fifth is 2 of the small pieces. Four fifths each give 2 pieces, which is 8 pieces out of 15:

<Claim display tex="\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 5} = \frac{8}{15}" />

The denominators multiply because the whole is cut into $3 \times 5$ pieces. The numerators multiply because you take $2 \times 4$ of them.

<Callout kind="idea">

To multiply fractions, multiply the numerators together and the denominators together, then simplify.

$$
\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}
$$

</Callout>

A whole number is a fraction with denominator 1. So three lots of two fifths is six fifths:

<Claim display tex="3 \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3}{1} \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{6}{5}" />

<Misconception id="multiply-top-and-bottom-by-whole" />

Simplifying can happen before you multiply. Any number on the top can cancel with any number on the bottom, because the order of multiplying does not matter:

<Claim display tex="\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{9} = \frac{3 \times 8}{4 \times 9} = \frac{24}{36} = \frac{2}{3}" />

Here 3 cancels with 9 and 4 cancels with 8, leaving $\frac{1 \times 2}{1 \times 3}$ directly. The sign rules from <Prereq id="s1-negative-operations" /> still apply: one negative fraction in a product makes the answer negative.

</Section>

<Section id="reciprocals" title="Reciprocals">

The <Term id="reciprocal" /> of a number is what you multiply it by to get 1. For a fraction, it is the fraction turned upside down:

<Claim display tex="\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{6}{6} = 1" />

The reciprocal of a whole number such as 4 is one quarter, because <Claim tex="4 = \frac{4}{1}" />:

<Claim display tex="4 \times \frac{1}{4} = 1" />

</Section>

<Section id="dividing" title="Dividing fractions">

Division asks how many of one amount fit into another. How many halves fit into 3? Each whole holds 2 halves, so 3 wholes hold 6:

<Claim display tex="3 \div \frac{1}{2} = 3 \times 2 = 6" />

Dividing by one half gave the same answer as multiplying by 2, its reciprocal. That is always true. Dividing by a number and multiplying by its reciprocal are the same operation, because both undo multiplying by that number.

<Callout kind="idea">

To divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal: keep the first fraction, change the division to a multiplication, and turn the second fraction upside down.

$$
\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}
$$

</Callout>

<Claim display tex="\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{9}{10} = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{10}{9} = \frac{30}{36} = \frac{5}{6}" />

You can check a division by multiplying back. The <Term id="quotient" /> times the number you divided by should give the starting number, here as a <Term id="product" />:

<Claim display tex="\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{9}{10} = \frac{3}{4}" />

<Practice />

<Takeaways>

- To multiply fractions, multiply the tops together and the bottoms together; no common denominator is needed.
- A whole number is a fraction with denominator 1. Multiplying it by a fraction multiplies the numerator only.
- The reciprocal of a fraction is the fraction turned upside down; a number times its reciprocal is 1.
- To divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal: turn the second fraction upside down, never the first.
- Cancel common factors between any top and any bottom to keep the numbers small, and simplify the answer.

</Takeaways>
