<WhyItMatters>

Roots and powers look like two separate ideas. This topic shows they are one idea. Once a root can be written as an exponent, a single set of laws handles both, and formulas with roots in them become much easier to rearrange and simplify.

Fractional exponents appear in formulas from science and finance, and they are the starting point for exponential growth, logarithms and, later, calculus, where exponents of every size are used all the time.

</WhyItMatters>

<Explanation>

<Section id="what-you-already-know" title="What you already know">

An <Term id="exponent" /> counts how many copies of the <Term id="base" /> are multiplied together:

<Claim display tex="2^{3} = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8" />

From <Prereq id="s2-exponent-laws" /> you have two laws. Multiplying powers of the same base adds the exponents, and a power of a power multiplies them:

$$
a^{m} \times a^{n} = a^{m + n} \qquad \left(a^{m}\right)^{n} = a^{mn}
$$

For example:

<Claim display tex="2^{2} \times 2^{3} = 2^{5}" />

<Claim display tex="\left(2^{2}\right)^{3} = 2^{6}" />

From <Prereq id="s2-zero-negative-exponents" />, a negative exponent means one over the power, its <Term id="reciprocal" />:

<Claim display tex="2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^{3}} = \frac{1}{8}" />

And from <Prereq id="s2-roots" />, a <Term id="square-root" /> undoes squaring, a <Term id="cube-root" /> undoes cubing, and in general an <Term id="nth-root">nth root</Term> undoes raising to the power n:

<Claim display tex="\sqrt{9} = 3" />

<Claim display tex="\sqrt[3]{8} = 2" />

<Callout kind="idea">

Those laws were discovered with whole-number exponents. The question for this topic is: what could an exponent such as one half possibly mean, if the same laws are to keep working?

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="unit-fraction-exponents" title="What an exponent of one half must mean">

Suppose $9^{\frac{1}{2}}$ is some number. We do not yet know which. Multiply it by itself. The law for multiplying powers says to add the exponents, and one half plus one half is one:

<Claim
  display
  tex="9^{\frac{1}{2}} \times 9^{\frac{1}{2}} = 9^{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}} = 9^{1} = 9"
/>

So $9^{\frac{1}{2}}$ is a number that, multiplied by itself, gives 9. That is exactly what the square root of 9 is. There is no other choice that keeps the law true:

<Claim display tex="9^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{9} = 3" />

The same reasoning works for a third. Three copies of $8^{\frac{1}{3}}$ multiply to give $8^{1}$, because three thirds make one:

<Claim display tex="8^{\frac{1}{3}} \times 8^{\frac{1}{3}} \times 8^{\frac{1}{3}} = 8^{1} = 8" />

So $8^{\frac{1}{3}}$ is the number that, used three times as a factor, gives 8. That is the cube root:

<Claim display tex="8^{\frac{1}{3}} = \sqrt[3]{8} = 2" />

<Callout kind="idea">

The rule: an exponent of one over n means the nth root.

$$
a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}
$$

</Callout>

Another way to see it is to climb from $a^{0}$ to $a^{1}$ in equal steps of the exponent. Each step must multiply by the same number, and n equal steps must multiply 1 up to a. Choose a base and a number of steps:

<Callout kind="note">

In this topic the base is always a positive number. Square roots of negative numbers do not exist among the numbers you have met so far, so a positive base keeps every step meaningful.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="general-fractional-exponents" title="The numerator and the denominator">

Now the exponent can be any fraction, such as two thirds. Two thirds is $2 \times \frac{1}{3}$, and a power of a power multiplies exponents. So we can take the cube root first, then square:

<Claim display tex="8^{\frac{2}{3}} = \left(8^{\frac{1}{3}}\right)^{2} = 2^{2} = 4" />

Two thirds is also $\frac{1}{3} \times 2$, so we could square first, then take the cube root. The answer is the same:

<Claim display tex="8^{\frac{2}{3}} = \left(8^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}} = \sqrt[3]{64} = 4" />

<Callout kind="idea">

The denominator is the root. The numerator is the power.

$$
a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}}
$$

</Callout>

Both orders give the same answer, but taking the root first keeps the numbers small. Compare the two routes for $27^{\frac{2}{3}}$. Root first:

<Claim display tex="27^{\frac{2}{3}} = \left(\sqrt[3]{27}\right)^{2} = 3^{2} = 9" />

Power first means finding the cube root of a much larger number:

<Claim display tex="27^{2} = 729" />

<Claim display tex="\sqrt[3]{729} = 9" />

</Section>

<Section id="negative-fractional-exponents" title="Negative fractional exponents">

A minus sign in the exponent still means the reciprocal, exactly as with whole-number exponents. Deal with it first, then use the root and the power:

<Claim display tex="27^{-\frac{2}{3}} = \frac{1}{27^{\frac{2}{3}}} = \frac{1}{9}" />

When the base is a fraction, the reciprocal is the fraction turned upside down:

<Claim
  display
  tex="\left(\frac{4}{9}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}} = \left(\frac{9}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{3}{2}"
/>

<Section id="radical-and-exponent-form" title="Radical form and exponent form">

The same quantity can be written in <Term id="radical-form" />, with a root sign, or in <Term id="exponent-form" />, with a fractional exponent. Moving between them uses only the rule above. Here, as before, the letter stands for a positive number:

<Claim display tex="x^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{x}" />

<Claim display tex="x^{\frac{3}{4}} = \sqrt[4]{x^{3}}" />

<Claim display tex="\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}} = x^{-\frac{2}{3}}" />

Exponent form is the one the laws work with, so it is usually the form to use when simplifying. Radical form is often how a question or a formula is written, so you need to read both.

<Practice />

<Takeaways>

- An exponent of one over n means the nth root: $a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}$.
- In any fractional exponent, the denominator is the root and the numerator is the power: $a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}}$.
- Take the root first. It keeps the numbers small.
- A negative exponent means the reciprocal: $a^{-\frac{m}{n}} = \frac{1}{a^{\frac{m}{n}}}$.
- An exponent is never a multiplier, and the root sign takes the denominator, not the numerator.

</Takeaways>
